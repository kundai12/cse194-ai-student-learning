How does AI affect student learning?

AI tools are already part of many students' schoolwork. They can explain concepts, summarize readings, help with research, edit writing, and solve problems. These uses can save time and make support easier to access.

At the same time, completing a task is not the same as learning from it. A student may produce a stronger answer with AI while remembering less, practicing less, or becoming less able to complete a similar task alone.

This project looks at both sides. It summarizes recent surveys and learning studies, explains what each source can and cannot show, and focuses on a practical question: What kinds of AI help keep the student involved in the thinking?

The studies below examine different populations and tasks. Percentages from U.S. teens, UK undergraduates, and controlled experiments should not be treated as directly comparable.

Key data

These numbers describe how commonly students report using AI and how they view it. Survey responses do not directly measure learning.

54%

U.S. teens have used chatbots for schoolwork

In a survey of 1,458 U.S. teens ages 13-17, 54% said they had used AI chatbots to get help with schoolwork.

Source: Pew Research Center, How Teens Use and View AI (2026)

10%

Use chatbots for all or most of their schoolwork

One in ten U.S. teens said chatbots help with all or most of their schoolwork. Another 21% said chatbots help with some of it.

Source: Pew Research Center, How Teens Use and View AI (2026)

95%

UK undergraduates report using AI in some form

A survey of 1,054 full-time UK undergraduates found that 95% used AI in at least one way. This is UK data and should not be generalized to all students.

Source: HEPI, Student Generative AI Survey 2026

49%

Say AI improved their student experience

In the same HEPI survey, 49% said AI had improved their student experience. This is a self-reported opinion, not a test of knowledge or skill.

Source: HEPI, Student Generative AI Survey 2026

Reported ChatGPT use for schoolwork increased from 13% to 26%

Pew estimated that 13% of all U.S. teens had used ChatGPT for schoolwork in 2023. In its 2024 survey, that figure was 26%. These earlier surveys asked specifically about ChatGPT, while the 2026 report asks about AI chatbots more generally.

Sources: Pew 2023 and Pew 2025

What learning studies suggest

The evidence does not support a simple conclusion that AI is always helpful or always harmful. The result depends on the task and how the tool is used.

1. Guardrails can change the learning outcome

A high school mathematics experiment found that unrestricted AI help improved students' performance during practice but harmed later performance when the AI was removed. A tutor-style version with learning-focused guardrails largely avoided that harm.

Source: Bastani et al. (2025), Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning

Limit: About 1,000 students in one school and one subject.

2. Better work does not necessarily mean more learning

In a university writing study, students using ChatGPT improved their essays, but the study did not find significantly higher knowledge gain or transfer. The authors warn that students may rely on the tool instead of monitoring and regulating their own learning.

Source: Fan et al. (2025), Beware of metacognitive laziness

Limit: 117 university students completing one writing task.

3. The type of help matters

A programming-course study found that asking AI for explanations supported understanding more than asking it to generate exercise solutions. This suggests that AI may be more useful when it supports a student's reasoning instead of replacing it.

Source: Lehmann, Cornelius, and Sting (2025), AI Meets the Classroom

Limit: Programming courses only; the cited version is a preprint.

4. Some possible cognitive effects remain uncertain

An essay-writing preprint compared participants using ChatGPT, search engines, or no tool and reported differences in brain connectivity and writing behavior. The study is useful as an early warning, but it does not prove permanent or long-term harm.

Source: Kosmyna et al. (2025), Your Brain on ChatGPT

Limit: Small sample, short writing task, and fewer participants in the final session.

Simple takeaways for students

  • Make your own attempt before asking AI to solve the problem.
  • Ask for a hint, explanation, example, or question instead of a finished answer.
  • Explain the answer back in your own words.
  • Check important facts, calculations, quotations, and citations.
  • After using AI, try a similar task without it.
  • Follow the AI policy for the specific course and assignment.

These are practical suggestions based on patterns in the research, not a validated scoring system or a guarantee of learning.
